Baizora Score: A Historical Backtest

Hypothetical & Backtested Data · Not A Live Track Record · Not Investment Advice

Read this before the numbers below. This page replays the Baizora Score formula against past price data to see what would have happened, hypothetically, on days the score crossed a given threshold. It is a backtest of historical data — not a live track record, not a strategy, and not a recommendation to buy, hold, or sell any security.

The window tested (Aug 2023 – Aug 2026) was a strong bull market for US large-cap equities, which mechanically favors trend-following signals like this one — it has not been tested through a market downturn. Past patterns are not predictive of future results. See Limitations below before drawing any conclusion from this data.

01 — What This Backtest Does

Replaying the score formula against history

The Baizora Score combines five component signals, each already computed daily on the live dashboard:

RS (Relative Strength) — 30% // percentile rank, price momentum vs. universe Momentum — 25% // percentile rank, shorter-horizon acceleration Breakout — 20% // today's price/volume vs. 21-day average Trend Consistency — 15% // share of 6 timeframes currently positive Volume Pressure — 10% // volume intensity × price direction

To see how a given score threshold lined up with what happened afterward, the formula above was recomputed for every trading day over roughly the past three years — not just today — for 516 of the 518 current S&P 500 + Nasdaq-100 constituents (two tickers were excluded on a data-format mismatch), using daily price bars as the input. A day only counts if the ticker already had a full year of prior trading history, so early listings and thin-history tickers are excluded rather than scored on partial data. Each scored day was then matched against that ticker's actual price change over the following 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months.

This produced 380,044 individual (date, ticker, score) observations spanning 2023-08-16 to 2026-08-14.

02 — Limitations (Read First)

Why these numbers need context

  • Single, favorable market regime. The entire backtest window is one continuous bull run. Roughly half the score's weight (RS + Momentum) is explicitly trend-following, so it is structurally likely to look better while trends persist. This backtest says nothing about how the score would have behaved in 2022, 2008, or any other drawdown — that data isn't in this window.
  • Not independent samples. The same ticker is scored on consecutive days, and the forward-return windows overlap heavily from one day to the next. The observation counts below are ticker-days, not independent bets — they should be read descriptively, not as a formally significance-tested result.
  • Return distributions are right-skewed. A handful of large winners can pull an average well above what a "typical" observation looked like. Median figures are shown alongside means for this reason and are generally the more representative number.
  • Universe survivorship. Today's S&P 500 / Nasdaq-100 constituent list was applied across the whole historical window, rather than each date's actual historical membership — a standard simplification that introduces mild survivorship bias.
  • Sample size shrinks fast at higher thresholds. The 70+ bucket has a reasonably deep sample; 90+ narrows to under 600 observations with a full 12-month forward window, which is thin enough that its numbers should be treated as directional at best.

03b — Does a Low Score Predict Decline?

The score is not symmetric

The honest read: this is a weak, inconsistent downside signal — nowhere near the strength of the upside one. At medium horizons (3–9 months) a low score does show a real, small negative tilt (roughly −0.4pp to −2pp median edge). But it fades and even reverses positive by 12 months, and win rate stays a majority 56–66% at every single horizon — loss rate never comes close to 50%, let alone exceeding it. Compare the magnitude: score ≥80's 12-month median edge is +17.0pp; score ≤30's is −0.6pp. The upside signal is roughly 30× larger.

This isn't a flaw so much as a structural property: every component in this score (RS, Momentum, Breakout, Trend, Volume Pressure) measures the presence of strength, not the presence of weakness. A low score means those strength signals aren't firing — it does not mean the opposite signals (breakdown, distribution, sustained deterioration) are. Recombining these same "how-good-is-it" ingredients in other ways (tested against BaizPersist and BaizConviction, both described below) doesn't change this — their low ends show the same weak, fading pattern, not a cleaner one. Read a low score as "not currently showing strength," not as "predicted to decline."

03d — BaizConviction — Results

Does rewarding consistency, not just today's score, help?

BaizConviction = round(sqrt(BaizScore × BaizPersist)), where BaizPersist is a ticker's own trailing 21-session (~1 month) average BaizScore. It's designed to reward stocks that are both strong today and have been consistently strong recently, rather than a single-day spike — both are null until a ticker has a month of history. Because BaizConviction has no natural ≥70/≥80/≥90 cutoffs of its own, it's compared against BaizScore at matched sample size: for every BaizScore threshold, the BaizConviction cutoff was chosen so both buckets contain (almost) exactly the same number of observations — an apples-to-apples test of whether the same-size bucket, defined differently, performs better.

Same bucket size, same horizon, same methodology — BaizConviction's 12-month median edge is meaningfully larger at both tiers tested (+9.3pp vs. +7.7pp at the ≥70-equivalent size; +24.1pp vs. +16.2pp at the ≥80-equivalent size). That's the one score-vs-score comparison on this page controlled for sample size rather than just threshold — still one backtest, one market regime, and not a guarantee it holds going forward.

04 — Testing Alternate Weightings

How the current weighting compares

Five other weighting schemes were recombined from the same underlying component scores and run through the identical backtest above. This tests whether the specific weighting matters, not whether the score "works" — the same regime-dependence and small-sample caveats above apply throughout.

For reference, the equivalent mean-based edges run roughly 2–3× larger throughout (e.g. current's ≥90 edge is +67.9pp by mean vs. +22.8pp by median) — a direct illustration of how much the skew in Section 03 was inflating the mean figures.

What this comparison suggests, cautiously: no single weighting scheme led at every threshold under median return either — current is effectively tied with equal-weight at 70+ (+7.8pp vs. +7.9pp), leads narrowly at 80+ (+17.0pp vs. momentum-amplified's +16.5pp), and is clearly behind momentum-amplified at the thin 90+ tier (+22.8pp vs. +28.8pp). The two schemes that reduced Breakout and Volume Pressure to near-zero weight remained the weakest at every threshold under median too — trend-consistency's 70+ edge nearly disappears (+1.6pp), and one of its horizons (3-month) is actually negative under median (see raw data), a sharper signal than the mean-based comparison gave that those two components carry real information rather than being redundant with RS/Momentum. Given the caveats above, this remains one data point toward future weighting review, not a conclusion to act on.

The momentum-amplified weighting above is what shipped to the live dashboard as BaizMomentum — see its results section above for the full comparison against BaizScore.

05 — Conclusion

Where this leaves the Baizora Score family

Across every test on this page — raw thresholds, persistence, alternate weightings, and the two derived scores — the same shape holds: a sustained high score is associated with stronger subsequent returns than baseline, the effect is concentrated in sustained strength rather than a single day's crossing, and the effect is asymmetric — a low score is a weak, unreliable signal for decline, not a mirror image of the upside signal. BaizMomentum and BaizConviction are offered as additional lenses on the same underlying data, not replacements for BaizScore, and BaizConviction is the one derived score that showed a clear edge over plain BaizScore when compared at matched sample size rather than just at a threshold.

None of this is a guarantee. Every number above comes from one backtest, over one continuous bull-market window, on survivorship-adjusted historical data — see Limitations for the full list of caveats before drawing any conclusion.
